# Logical Fallacy Reference

Comprehensive reference of 136 logical fallacies for detection in text and audio transcripts. Source taxonomy adapted from Wikipedia's *List of Fallacies*, cross-checked against the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, and Fallacy Files.

Each entry includes: definition, abstract logical pattern, a clean textbook-style example, and a conversational/naturalistic example closer to how the fallacy appears in speech or informal writing.

## Formal: Formal Fallacy

### Appeal to Probability
**Definition:** Taking something for granted because it would probably be the case, or might possibly be the case.

**Pattern:** `X would probably happen; therefore X is true/assumed.`

**Textbook example:** Since most cars in this lot are red, the car that was stolen was probably red.

**Conversational example:** I mean, statistically most break-ins happen at night, so it definitely happened at night.

### Argument from Fallacy
*Also known as: Fallacy Fallacy*

**Definition:** Assuming that if a particular argument for a conclusion is fallacious, the conclusion itself must be false.

**Pattern:** `Argument for X is fallacious; therefore X is false.`

**Textbook example:** Your argument for God's existence was a logical fallacy, so God must not exist.

**Conversational example:** That study you cited had a cherry-picking problem, so vaccines obviously don't work.

### Base Rate Fallacy
**Definition:** Making a probability judgment based on conditional probabilities without accounting for prior (base rate) probabilities.

**Pattern:** `P(evidence|hypothesis) is high; therefore P(hypothesis|evidence) is high, ignoring base rates.`

**Textbook example:** The test is 99% accurate, so if you tested positive for the rare disease, you almost certainly have it.

**Conversational example:** He failed the lie detector, so he's basically guilty — those things are like 90% accurate.

### Conjunction Fallacy
**Definition:** Assuming that an outcome satisfying multiple conditions simultaneously is more probable than an outcome satisfying just one of them.

**Pattern:** `P(A and B) judged > P(A).`

**Textbook example:** Linda is a feminist and a bank teller is judged more likely than Linda is a bank teller.

**Conversational example:** It's more likely he's a quiet, brilliant programmer who also plays chess than just that he's a programmer.

### Masked-Man Fallacy
*Also known as: Illicit Substitution of Identicals*

**Definition:** Substituting identical designators in a true statement can lead to a false one, due to differing epistemic contexts.

**Pattern:** `A=B is true; I know A; therefore I know B (fails if A=B is not known).`

**Textbook example:** I know who my best friend is. The masked man is my best friend (unknown to me). Therefore I know who the masked man is.

**Conversational example:** You said you trust the CEO. The CEO is the leaker. So you trust the leaker — but you didn't know that.

## Formal: Propositional Fallacy

### Affirming a Disjunct
**Definition:** Concluding that one disjunct must be false because the other disjunct is true, in an inclusive 'or' statement.

**Pattern:** `A or B; A; therefore not B.`

**Textbook example:** It's either raining or cloudy. It's raining. Therefore it's not cloudy.

**Conversational example:** She's either tired or annoyed. She's clearly tired, so she can't be annoyed too.

### Affirming the Consequent
**Definition:** Claiming the antecedent of a conditional is true because the consequent is true.

**Pattern:** `If A, then B; B; therefore A.`

**Textbook example:** If it rains, the ground is wet. The ground is wet. Therefore it rained.

**Conversational example:** If he's lying, he'd avoid eye contact. He's avoiding eye contact, so he must be lying.

### Denying the Antecedent
**Definition:** Claiming the consequent of a conditional is false because the antecedent is false.

**Pattern:** `If A, then B; not A; therefore not B.`

**Textbook example:** If you study, you'll pass. You didn't study. Therefore you won't pass.

**Conversational example:** If we cut taxes, the economy grows. We're not cutting taxes, so the economy won't grow.

## Formal: Quantification Fallacy

### Existential Fallacy
**Definition:** A syllogism with a universal premise but a particular conclusion, which illegitimately assumes existence.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; therefore some A exist that are B.`

**Textbook example:** All trespassers will be prosecuted; therefore some trespassers exist.

**Conversational example:** All unicorns have horns, so there must be some unicorns out there with horns.

## Formal: Syllogistic Fallacy

### Affirmative Conclusion from a Negative Premise
*Also known as: Illicit Negative*

**Definition:** A categorical syllogism reaches a positive conclusion despite having at least one negative premise.

**Pattern:** `No A are B; some C are A; therefore some C are B.`

**Textbook example:** No fish are mammals. Some pets are fish. Therefore some pets are mammals.

**Conversational example:** None of the interns finished early, and some interns are juniors, so some juniors finished early.

### Fallacy of Exclusive Premises
**Definition:** An invalid categorical syllogism in which both premises are negative.

**Pattern:** `No A are B; no B are C; therefore (invalid conclusion about A and C).`

**Textbook example:** No cats are dogs. No dogs are birds. Therefore no cats are birds (invalid form, even if true).

**Conversational example:** No managers signed off, and no executives reviewed it, so clearly nobody approved it.

### Fallacy of Four Terms
*Also known as: Quaternio Terminorum*

**Definition:** A categorical syllogism that improperly contains four distinct terms instead of three.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; all C are D; therefore all A are D.`

**Textbook example:** All humans are mortal. All Greeks are rational. Therefore all humans are rational.

**Conversational example:** Engineers are logical. Doctors are caring. So engineers must be caring.

### Illicit Major
**Definition:** A categorical syllogism invalid because its major term is undistributed in the major premise but distributed in the conclusion.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; no C are A; therefore no C are B.`

**Textbook example:** All dogs are mammals. No cats are dogs. Therefore no cats are mammals.

**Conversational example:** All our top performers are remote. None of the new hires are remote. So none of the new hires are top performers.

### Illicit Minor
**Definition:** A categorical syllogism invalid because its minor term is undistributed in the minor premise but distributed in the conclusion.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; all A are C; therefore all C are B.`

**Textbook example:** All squares are rectangles. All squares are quadrilaterals. Therefore all quadrilaterals are rectangles.

**Conversational example:** All our best clients pay on time. All our best clients are local. So all local clients pay on time.

### Negative Conclusion from Affirmative Premises
*Also known as: Illicit Affirmative*

**Definition:** A categorical syllogism with a negative conclusion despite having only affirmative premises.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; all B are C; therefore some C are not A.`

**Textbook example:** All roses are flowers. All flowers are plants. Therefore some plants are not roses (drawn as a negative when premises were affirmative).

**Conversational example:** All our products are tested, and all tested items are logged, so some logged items aren't our products.

### Fallacy of the Undistributed Middle
**Definition:** The middle term in a categorical syllogism is never distributed, making the syllogism invalid.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; all C are B; therefore all A are C.`

**Textbook example:** All dogs are animals. All cats are animals. Therefore all dogs are cats.

**Conversational example:** All our competitors raised prices, and we raised prices too, so we must be acting like our competitors.

## Formal: Modal Fallacy

### Modal Fallacy
**Definition:** Confusing what is necessary for an outcome with what is sufficient for it.

**Pattern:** `X is necessary for Y; therefore X is sufficient for Y (or vice versa).`

**Textbook example:** Oxygen is necessary for fire, so wherever there is oxygen there must be fire.

**Conversational example:** You need funding to launch a startup, so if you have funding, the startup will launch.

### Modal Scope Fallacy
**Definition:** Placing an unwarranted degree of necessity onto the conclusion that wasn't present in the premises.

**Pattern:** `Necessarily (if A, then B); A; therefore necessarily B.`

**Textbook example:** It is necessarily true that if you're a bachelor, you're unmarried. You're a bachelor. Therefore you're necessarily unmarried (conflating logical necessity with the modal status of the conclusion).

**Conversational example:** If the data's accurate, the model must be right, and since the data could theoretically be accurate, the model has to be right.

## Informal: Improper Presumption

### Argument from Incredulity
*Also known as: Appeal to Common Sense, Divine Fallacy*

**Definition:** Concluding something is false because one cannot imagine it being true, or true because one cannot imagine it being false.

**Pattern:** `I cannot imagine how X could be true; therefore X is false.`

**Textbook example:** I can't see how the universe could have just appeared from nothing, so something must have created it.

**Conversational example:** There's no way she figured that out on her own — I just can't picture it, so someone must've helped her.

### Argument to Moderation
*Also known as: False Compromise, Middle Ground Fallacy, Argumentum ad Temperantiam*

**Definition:** Assuming that the compromise between two positions is always correct.

**Pattern:** `Position A; position B; therefore the truth is the midpoint between A and B.`

**Textbook example:** Some say the earth is round, others say it's flat — the truth is probably that it's somewhat round.

**Conversational example:** You think we should fire him, I think we should give him a raise — let's just split the difference and keep his pay the same.

### Continuum Fallacy
*Also known as: Fallacy of the Beard, Sorites Fallacy, Line-Drawing Fallacy*

**Definition:** Improperly rejecting a claim because it is vague or imprecise at the margins, despite being clear in central cases.

**Pattern:** `There's no precise cutoff between A and not-A; therefore the distinction between A and not-A doesn't exist.`

**Textbook example:** There's no exact moment a person becomes 'old,' so the concept of old age is meaningless.

**Conversational example:** Nobody can say exactly when 'a lot of meetings' becomes 'too many meetings,' so you can't really say we have too many.

### Suppressed Correlative
**Definition:** Redefining a correlative term so that one of the alternatives becomes impossible.

**Pattern:** `Redefine X relative to Y such that not-X becomes unattainable by definition.`

**Textbook example:** I'm not fat because I'm thinner than John.

**Conversational example:** I'm not really 'late' for things — I just show up whenever I show up, and that's when it starts for me.

### Double Counting
**Definition:** Counting events or occurrences more than once in probabilistic reasoning, inflating the apparent probability.

**Pattern:** `P(A) + P(A again under a different label) summed as if independent.`

**Textbook example:** Counting the probability of rain on Saturday AND the probability of rain this weekend as separate, additive risks.

**Conversational example:** We've got the budget risk, the timeline risk, and the 'budget timeline' risk — that's three separate things to worry about.

### Ecological Fallacy
**Definition:** Inferring something about an individual based solely on aggregate statistics about the group they belong to.

**Pattern:** `Group G has average property P; individual X belongs to G; therefore X has property P.`

**Textbook example:** This neighborhood has a high average income, so that person living there must be wealthy.

**Conversational example:** Engineers at this company tend to be introverted, so he's probably pretty quiet.

### Equivocation
**Definition:** Using a word with more than one meaning in an argument without clarifying which meaning is intended, in a way that misleads.

**Pattern:** `X is A (meaning 1); A implies B (meaning 2); therefore X is B.`

**Textbook example:** A feather is light. What is light cannot be dark. Therefore a feather cannot be dark.

**Conversational example:** He said he's 'open' to feedback, so clearly he's an open book about everything in his life.

### Ambiguous Middle Term
**Definition:** A syllogism's middle term is used with two different meanings across the premises.

**Pattern:** `All A are B(meaning 1); all B(meaning 2) are C; therefore all A are C.`

**Textbook example:** Power corrupts. Knowledge is power. Therefore knowledge corrupts.

**Conversational example:** Banks are secure. The data is in the cloud bank. Therefore the data is secure.

### Motte-and-Bailey Fallacy
**Definition:** Conflating an easily-defended modest position (the motte) with a more controversial one (the bailey), retreating to the former when challenged.

**Pattern:** `Assert controversial claim B; when challenged, defend only modest claim A as if it were B.`

**Textbook example:** Claiming 'all cultures are equally valid' (bailey), but when challenged, retreating to 'we shouldn't judge other cultures unfairly' (motte).

**Conversational example:** He says 'the system is rigged against us,' but when pressed he just means 'sometimes things are unfair' — much easier to defend.

### Fallacy of Accent
**Definition:** Changing the meaning of a statement by shifting which word is emphasized, without making the shift explicit.

**Pattern:** `Statement S with stress on word W1 means X; restated with stress on W2, S now implies Y.`

**Textbook example:** 'We should not speak ill of our friends' can imply it's fine to speak ill of others, depending on stress.

**Conversational example:** He said 'I didn't say she stole it' — emphasis on 'I' implies someone else said it, which he later used to deny the accusation entirely.

### Persuasive Definition
**Definition:** Presenting a biased or unusual definition of a term as though it were the standard, commonly accepted one.

**Pattern:** `Redefine term T with built-in evaluative spin; argue using T as if neutrally defined.`

**Textbook example:** Defining 'freedom' as 'doing whatever benefits society' to argue that restricting individual choice is actually freedom.

**Conversational example:** He calls it 'just being direct,' but what he means by 'direct' is being rude without consequences.

### Etymological Fallacy
**Definition:** Assuming a word's original or historical meaning must govern or match its present-day usage.

**Pattern:** `Word W originally meant M; therefore W must still mean M today.`

**Textbook example:** 'Decimate' originally meant 'reduce by a tenth,' so using it to mean 'destroy almost entirely' is wrong.

**Conversational example:** 'Awful' used to mean 'awe-inspiring,' so technically calling something awful should be a compliment.

### Fallacy of Composition
**Definition:** Assuming that something true of the parts of a whole must also be true of the whole itself.

**Pattern:** `Each part of X has property P; therefore X (the whole) has property P.`

**Textbook example:** Every player on the team is excellent, so the team must be excellent.

**Conversational example:** Each line of the budget looks reasonable, so the whole budget must be reasonable.

### Fallacy of Division
**Definition:** Assuming that something true of a whole must also be true of its individual parts.

**Pattern:** `X (the whole) has property P; therefore each part of X has property P.`

**Textbook example:** This company is highly profitable, so every department in it must be profitable.

**Conversational example:** Our team hit its targets this quarter, so everyone on the team must have hit their individual targets.

### False Attribution
**Definition:** Appealing to an irrelevant, unqualified, biased, or fabricated source to support an argument.

**Pattern:** `Source S (unreliable/irrelevant) says X; therefore X is true.`

**Textbook example:** A blog with no credentials claims the new policy will fail, so it must be a bad policy.

**Conversational example:** My cousin's friend who works in tech says AI is definitely going to replace all jobs by next year.

### Fallacy of Quoting Out of Context
*Also known as: Contextomy, Quotation Mining*

**Definition:** Selectively excerpting words from their original context in a way that distorts their intended meaning.

**Pattern:** `Quote fragment F from source S, omitting context C that reverses F's meaning.`

**Textbook example:** Cutting 'I don't think this plan will fail' down to 'this plan will fail.'

**Conversational example:** He said 'I'm not saying it's impossible, but it's extremely unlikely' and they reported it as 'CEO admits it's impossible.'

### False Authority
*Also known as: Single Authority*

**Definition:** Citing an expert of dubious or irrelevant credentials, or relying on a single opinion, to support a claim.

**Pattern:** `Person P (not a relevant expert) says X; therefore X is true.`

**Textbook example:** A famous actor endorses a diet supplement as medically effective.

**Conversational example:** My personal trainer says this investment strategy is solid, so I'm going all in.

### False Dilemma
*Also known as: False Dichotomy, Black-or-White Fallacy, Bifurcation*

**Definition:** Presenting only two options as if they were the only possibilities when more exist.

**Pattern:** `Either A or B; not A; therefore B (when C, D, etc. are also possible).`

**Textbook example:** You're either with us or against us.

**Conversational example:** Either we cut the whole team's budget or the project fails completely — those are our only options.

### False Equivalence
**Definition:** Describing two or more things as essentially equal when meaningful differences make them not comparable.

**Pattern:** `A and B share property P; therefore A and B are equivalent.`

**Textbook example:** Both candidates have made misleading statements, so they're equally dishonest.

**Conversational example:** He was 5 minutes late once and she's been late every day this month, but you're treating it like the same problem.

### Historian's Fallacy
**Definition:** Assuming that decision-makers in the past had the same information available as those who later analyze their decisions.

**Pattern:** `We now know X; therefore the historical actor should have known X and acted accordingly.`

**Textbook example:** Judging a 19th-century general for not anticipating a tactic that was only understood decades later.

**Conversational example:** Why didn't the team pivot earlier — it's so obvious now that the market was shifting.

### Historical Fallacy
**Definition:** Believing a result occurred only because a specific process was performed, when the process may be unrelated to the outcome.

**Pattern:** `Process P was performed; outcome O occurred; therefore P caused O.`

**Textbook example:** We followed the exact same five-step ritual before our last win, so we must repeat it to win again.

**Conversational example:** We did the all-hands meeting right before the deal closed, so the meeting must be why it closed.

### Homunculus Fallacy
**Definition:** Explaining a concept by inserting a smaller, unexplained version of the same concept, without real explanatory progress.

**Pattern:** `X is explained by a smaller X-like agent inside the system, which itself goes unexplained.`

**Textbook example:** Thoughts are produced by a little 'thinker' inside the brain who does the thinking.

**Conversational example:** The model 'understands' the prompt because there's an internal reasoning module that understands it.

### Inflation of Conflict
**Definition:** Arguing that because experts disagree on a point, no conclusion can be reached or the field itself is illegitimate.

**Pattern:** `Expert A and Expert B disagree on X; therefore X is unknowable / the field is bunk.`

**Textbook example:** Nutritionists disagree about the ideal diet, so nutrition science is worthless.

**Conversational example:** Economists can't even agree on inflation forecasts, so the whole field is basically guesswork.

### If-by-Whiskey
**Definition:** Supporting both sides of an issue at once by using emotionally loaded, ambiguous language tailored to each audience.

**Pattern:** `If you mean X (negative framing), I oppose it; if you mean X (positive framing), I support it — said to please everyone.`

**Textbook example:** A politician's classic 'if by whiskey' speech supporting and opposing alcohol depending on the listener's leaning.

**Conversational example:** If 'flexible hours' means people slacking off, I'm against it; if it means trusting professionals, I'm all for it — so vote however you want.

### Incomplete Comparison
**Definition:** Making a comparison without providing enough information to evaluate it meaningfully.

**Pattern:** `X is better/more than Y (comparison basis unspecified or missing).`

**Textbook example:** This new phone is 50% faster!

**Conversational example:** Our new plan saves you more money — without saying more than what, or under what conditions.

### Kettle Logic
**Definition:** Using multiple jointly inconsistent arguments to defend the same position.

**Pattern:** `Defend X using argument A; also defend X using argument B, where A and B contradict each other.`

**Textbook example:** I never borrowed your kettle. It was already broken when I borrowed it. Also, I returned it in perfect condition.

**Conversational example:** I wasn't even at the meeting, and besides, what I said there was taken out of context, and anyway everyone agreed with me.

### Ludic Fallacy
**Definition:** Failing to account for unknown unknowns and real-world randomness when applying simplified probabilistic models.

**Pattern:** `Model M assigns probability P to event E (ignoring unmodeled real-world risk factors).`

**Textbook example:** A risk model based on historical data fails to predict a market crash caused by an unprecedented event.

**Conversational example:** Our simulation says there's only a 1% chance of failure, so we're basically guaranteed to be fine.

### Lump of Labour Fallacy
**Definition:** The misconception that there is a fixed amount of work in an economy, so automation or immigration necessarily reduces jobs for others.

**Pattern:** `Total work is fixed; if group A does work W, group B has less work available.`

**Textbook example:** Immigrants are taking jobs that would otherwise go to native workers.

**Conversational example:** If we automate this process, that's just fewer jobs for people, period.

### McNamara Fallacy
*Also known as: Quantitative Fallacy*

**Definition:** Making decisions based only on quantitative measures, discounting qualitative factors that resist measurement.

**Pattern:** `Metric M is high/low; therefore the decision is correct, ignoring unmeasured qualitative factors.`

**Textbook example:** Judging the success of a war solely by body counts, ignoring strategic and humanitarian context.

**Conversational example:** Her ticket-closing numbers are the highest on the team, so she's clearly our best support rep.

### Mind Projection Fallacy
**Definition:** Assuming a statement about an object describes an inherent property of the object rather than a feature of one's own perception.

**Pattern:** `I perceive X as P; therefore X inherently has property P.`

**Textbook example:** Calling a quantum particle's state 'uncertain' as if uncertainty is a property of the particle rather than of our knowledge.

**Conversational example:** This task feels impossible, so it must actually be impossible.

### Moving the Goalposts
*Also known as: Raising the Bar*

**Definition:** Dismissing evidence presented for a claim and demanding additional, often greater, evidence instead.

**Pattern:** `Provide evidence E for claim C; responder demands evidence E2 > E, repeatedly.`

**Textbook example:** Show me one peer-reviewed study. [Shown one.] Well, now show me ten, from different countries, over twenty years.

**Conversational example:** I showed you the receipts. Okay, now show me the bank statement too. And the email confirming it. And...

### Nirvana Fallacy
*Also known as: Perfect-Solution Fallacy*

**Definition:** Rejecting a solution because it is not perfect, ignoring that it may still be a meaningful improvement.

**Pattern:** `Solution S does not fully solve problem P; therefore S should be rejected.`

**Textbook example:** Seatbelts don't prevent all car deaths, so there's no point in requiring them.

**Conversational example:** This new process won't eliminate every bug, so why bother changing anything?

### Package Deal Fallacy
**Definition:** Treating essentially dissimilar concepts as though they necessarily go together.

**Pattern:** `X and Y are typically grouped together; therefore accepting X requires accepting Y.`

**Textbook example:** If you support free markets, you must also oppose all forms of regulation.

**Conversational example:** If you care about the environment, you have to support this specific policy too.

### Proof by Assertion
**Definition:** Repeating a claim over and over, regardless of contradiction, as if repetition establishes truth.

**Pattern:** `X (restated repeatedly, without new support).`

**Textbook example:** This product is the best — and again, it's simply the best, hands down the best.

**Conversational example:** I keep telling you, this plan will work. It will work. I'm telling you, it'll work.

### Prosecutor's Fallacy
**Definition:** Confusing the low probability of a false match with a low probability that a given match is false, ignoring the number of opportunities for a false match.

**Pattern:** `P(evidence|innocent) is low; therefore P(innocent|evidence) is low (ignoring population size/search space).`

**Textbook example:** The DNA match has a 1-in-a-million error rate, so this match must be correct — ignoring that millions of people were searched.

**Conversational example:** The algorithm flagged his transaction as fraud, and it's 99.9% accurate, so he's basically guilty.

### Proving Too Much
**Definition:** An argument's logic, if valid, would also prove an absurd or clearly false additional conclusion.

**Pattern:** `Argument for X also implies absurd Y by the same logic; therefore the argument is flawed.`

**Textbook example:** If 'it's natural so it's good' justified eating organic food, it would also justify diseases, which are natural too.

**Conversational example:** If 'we've always done it this way' justifies keeping this process, it'd also justify never updating any tool we use.

### Psychologist's Fallacy
**Definition:** An observer assumes their own perspective is objective when interpreting someone else's behavior.

**Pattern:** `I interpret behavior B as meaning M; therefore B objectively means M.`

**Textbook example:** A therapist assumes a patient's silence means resistance, without considering other explanations.

**Conversational example:** He didn't respond right away, so obviously he's avoiding the question.

### Reification
*Also known as: Concretism, Hypostatization, Misplaced Concreteness*

**Definition:** Treating an abstract concept or hypothetical construct as if it were a concrete, real entity with agency.

**Pattern:** `Abstract concept C is described as acting/choosing/wanting, as if C were a physical agent.`

**Textbook example:** Evolution 'decides' which traits survive.

**Conversational example:** The market 'wants' to correct itself, so we should just let it.

### Retrospective Determinism
**Definition:** Believing that because an event occurred under certain circumstances, those circumstances made the event inevitable.

**Pattern:** `Circumstance C preceded event E; therefore C made E inevitable.`

**Textbook example:** He won the lottery while wearing his lucky socks, so the socks made winning inevitable.

**Conversational example:** We launched on a Tuesday and it went viral, so Tuesday launches are clearly the move.

### Slippery Slope
*Also known as: Thin Edge of the Wedge, Camel's Nose*

**Definition:** Asserting that a small first step will inevitably lead to a chain of events ending in a significant negative outcome, without justifying the chain.

**Pattern:** `If A happens, then B, then C... then catastrophe Z; therefore A must not happen.`

**Textbook example:** If we allow students to retake one exam, soon no one will ever take exams seriously again.

**Conversational example:** If we let people work from home one day a week, next thing you know nobody comes into the office at all.

### Special Pleading
**Definition:** Claiming an exemption to a generally accepted rule without adequately justifying why the exemption applies.

**Pattern:** `Rule R applies generally; X claims to be exempt from R, without sufficient justification.`

**Textbook example:** Everyone should follow the deadline policy, except me, because my situation is different (unexplained).

**Conversational example:** Late work isn't accepted from anyone else, but in my case it should be, because... it's me.

### Begging the Question
*Also known as: Petitio Principii*

**Definition:** Using the conclusion of an argument, or an assumption equivalent to it, as one of its own premises.

**Pattern:** `X is true because Y, where Y presupposes X.`

**Textbook example:** Smoking is deadly because cigarettes can kill you, and anything that kills is deadly (defines 'deadly' into the premise).

**Conversational example:** Of course the policy is fair — it's fair because it treats everyone the same way, which is what fairness means, which is why it's fair.

### Circular Reasoning
*Also known as: Circulus in Demonstrando*

**Definition:** An argument whose conclusion is restated as, or directly depends on, one of its own premises.

**Pattern:** `X is true because Y; Y is true because X.`

**Textbook example:** The Bible is true because it's the word of God, and we know it's the word of God because the Bible says so.

**Conversational example:** He's guilty because he failed the test, and the test is accurate because guilty people fail it.

### Loaded Label
**Definition:** Using an evocative or connotation-laden term to sway a conclusion without directly arguing for it, a form of begging the question.

**Pattern:** `Describe X using term T (loaded with approval/disapproval) to imply a conclusion about X.`

**Textbook example:** Organic food is 'safe and healthy,' implying non-organic food is unsafe and unhealthy.

**Conversational example:** Our plan is the 'common sense' approach, implying any other plan is nonsensical.

### Fallacy of Many Questions
*Also known as: Complex Question, Loaded Question, Plurium Interrogationum*

**Definition:** Asking a question that presupposes something unproven or unaccepted, limiting any direct answer to serve the questioner's agenda.

**Pattern:** `Question Q presupposes unproven claim P; any direct answer to Q implicitly affirms P.`

**Textbook example:** Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?

**Conversational example:** So when did you start ignoring my emails?

## Informal: Faulty Generalization

### Accident
**Definition:** Applying a general rule to a specific case where an exception clearly should apply.

**Pattern:** `General rule R applies to most cases; case C is an exception; rule R is still applied to C.`

**Textbook example:** Cutting people with knives is illegal; therefore surgeons should be arrested for performing surgery.

**Conversational example:** Company policy says no exceptions for late arrivals, even though her train was cancelled.

### No True Scotsman
*Also known as: Appeal to Purity*

**Definition:** Making a generalization unfalsifiable by redefining it to exclude any counterexample as it arises.

**Pattern:** `All A are B; counterexample C is A but not B; therefore C isn't a 'real' A.`

**Textbook example:** No Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge. But my uncle Angus does. Well, no true Scotsman does.

**Conversational example:** Real fans never miss a game. But he missed one last week. Well, he's not a real fan then.

### Cherry Picking
*Also known as: Suppressed Evidence, Card Stacking*

**Definition:** Selecting only the data or cases that support a position while ignoring data that contradicts it.

**Pattern:** `Present supporting cases C1, C2, C3; omit contradicting cases C4, C5.`

**Textbook example:** Citing the three studies that support a drug's effectiveness while ignoring the twenty that don't.

**Conversational example:** He only brings up the quarters where revenue went up, never the ones where it dropped.

### Nut-Picking
**Definition:** Seeking out fringe, unrepresentative statements from members of an opposing group to portray the whole group as incompetent or irrational.

**Pattern:** `Find the most extreme statement from group G; present it as representative of G.`

**Textbook example:** Highlighting one extreme social media comment as representative of an entire political movement.

**Conversational example:** One guy on the forum said something crazy, so apparently that's what 'everyone in that community' believes.

### Survivorship Bias
**Definition:** Focusing on the successes of a process while ignoring the (often more numerous) failures, skewing perceived outcomes.

**Pattern:** `Successes S1...Sn are visible and promoted; failures are invisible/unmentioned; conclusion drawn from S only.`

**Textbook example:** Studying surviving WWII planes to decide where to add armor, ignoring that planes hit in other places never made it back.

**Conversational example:** Every successful founder I follow dropped out of college, so dropping out must be the key to success.

### False Analogy
**Definition:** Drawing a comparison between two things that are not sufficiently similar in the relevant respects.

**Pattern:** `A is like B in respect R1; B has property P; therefore A has property P (R1 insufficient to support P).`

**Textbook example:** A company is like a family, so employees should never be let go.

**Conversational example:** Running a household budget is just like running a national economy, so the government should never run a deficit.

### Hasty Generalization
*Also known as: Insufficient Sample, Secundum Quid, Jumping to Conclusions*

**Definition:** Drawing a broad conclusion from a sample that is too small or unrepresentative.

**Pattern:** `Sample S (small/unrepresentative) has property P; therefore the whole population has property P.`

**Textbook example:** I met two rude tourists from that country, so people from there must be rude.

**Conversational example:** Both candidates I interviewed from that school were unprepared, so that program must not teach well.

### Argument from Anecdote
**Definition:** Using a personal story or isolated example as if it were sufficient evidence for a general claim.

**Pattern:** `I/someone experienced X; therefore X is generally true.`

**Textbook example:** My grandfather smoked his whole life and lived to 95, so smoking isn't that dangerous.

**Conversational example:** My friend took that supplement and felt amazing, so it obviously works.

### Misleading Vividness
**Definition:** Describing an event in vivid, emotionally striking detail to make it seem more statistically significant than it is.

**Pattern:** `Vivid anecdote A described in detail; A treated as more probable/important than base rates suggest.`

**Textbook example:** After hearing one graphic story about a plane crash, refusing to fly despite air travel's overall safety.

**Conversational example:** After that one horror story about the surgery going wrong, she decided the whole procedure was too risky.

### Overwhelming Exception
**Definition:** A generalization technically holds but comes with so many qualifications that little of the original claim remains.

**Pattern:** `All A are B, except in cases C1, C2, C3... (which cover most actual instances).`

**Textbook example:** Everyone has an equal chance to succeed — except for income, location, education, and connections, which determine most outcomes.

**Conversational example:** The policy applies to everyone — well, except contractors, except remote staff, except anyone hired before 2020...

## Informal: Questionable Cause

### Cum Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
*Also known as: Correlation Implies Causation*

**Definition:** Assuming that because two variables are correlated, one must cause the other.

**Pattern:** `A correlates with B; therefore A causes B.`

**Textbook example:** Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer, so ice cream causes drowning.

**Conversational example:** Every time we run ads on Fridays, sales go up — the ads must be working (ignoring weekend shopping patterns).

### Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc
*Also known as: Temporal Sequence Implies Causation*

**Definition:** Assuming that because X happened before Y, X must have caused Y.

**Pattern:** `X occurred; then Y occurred; therefore X caused Y.`

**Textbook example:** I wore my lucky hat and we won the game, so the hat caused the win.

**Conversational example:** We switched vendors in March and revenue dipped in April, so the new vendor must be the problem.

### Wrong Direction
*Also known as: Reverse Causation*

**Definition:** Reversing cause and effect, claiming the actual effect is the cause and vice versa.

**Pattern:** `A causes B (claimed), when in reality B causes A.`

**Textbook example:** Claiming that carrying lighters causes smoking, when really smokers are the ones who carry lighters.

**Conversational example:** He says success made him confident, but actually his confidence is what helped him become successful.

### Ignoring a Common Cause
**Definition:** Concluding A causes B, or vice versa, while ignoring a third factor C that actually causes both.

**Pattern:** `A and B are correlated; concluded A causes B; in reality, C causes both A and B.`

**Textbook example:** Concluding that having a lighter causes lung disease, ignoring that smoking causes both.

**Conversational example:** Teams with more meetings ship slower, so meetings must be the problem — ignoring that complex projects cause both more meetings and slower shipping.

### Fallacy of the Single Cause
*Also known as: Causal Oversimplification*

**Definition:** Assuming a single, simple cause for an outcome that actually resulted from multiple contributing causes.

**Pattern:** `Outcome O occurred; cause C1 is identified; conclusion: C1 alone caused O (ignoring C2, C3...).`

**Textbook example:** The company failed because of one bad CEO decision, ignoring market conditions, competition, and internal structural issues.

**Conversational example:** The project failed because the deadline was too tight — never mind the unclear requirements and turnover.

### Furtive Fallacy
**Definition:** Assuming that significant outcomes must result from the deliberate malfeasance of decision-makers.

**Pattern:** `Bad outcome O occurred; therefore someone must have deliberately and secretly caused O.`

**Textbook example:** Assuming a stock market crash must be the result of a deliberate conspiracy rather than emergent systemic factors.

**Conversational example:** The product launch flopped — someone on the team must have sabotaged it on purpose.

### Magical Thinking
**Definition:** Believing that one's thoughts, rituals, or symbolic actions can directly cause unrelated real-world outcomes.

**Pattern:** `Performing ritual/thought R; therefore unrelated outcome O is more likely.`

**Textbook example:** Knocking on wood to prevent bad luck.

**Conversational example:** If I just visualize the deal closing hard enough, it'll happen.

### Observational Interpretation Fallacy
**Definition:** Misinterpreting an association found in an observational study as evidence of a causal relationship.

**Pattern:** `Observational study finds A associated with B; concluded A causes B.`

**Textbook example:** An observational study finds coffee drinkers live longer, so coffee is concluded to extend life.

**Conversational example:** The survey shows employees who use the new tool report higher satisfaction, so the tool must be causing the satisfaction.

### Regression Fallacy
**Definition:** Ascribing a cause to a change that is actually just natural statistical fluctuation (regression to the mean).

**Pattern:** `Extreme value V occurred; intervention I applied; value returns to normal; concluded I caused the return to normal.`

**Textbook example:** A struggling athlete gets a new coach, performance improves (regressing to their normal average), and the coach is credited.

**Conversational example:** Sales were unusually low last month, we did an emergency promo, and sales bounced back — the promo must have worked.

### Gambler's Fallacy
**Definition:** The mistaken belief that past independent random outcomes affect the probability of future independent outcomes.

**Pattern:** `Random event E has occurred N times in a row; therefore the opposite outcome is 'due' next.`

**Textbook example:** The roulette wheel landed on red 10 times in a row, so black is due next.

**Conversational example:** We've lost the last three pitches, so we're bound to win the next one.

### Inverse Gambler's Fallacy
**Definition:** Inferring from an unlikely outcome that the underlying random process must have run many times before.

**Pattern:** `Unlikely outcome O observed; therefore the process generating O must have occurred many times.`

**Textbook example:** Seeing someone roll double sixes and assuming they must have been rolling dice for a long time before you arrived.

**Conversational example:** This algorithm found such a perfect match, it must have run through thousands of attempts behind the scenes.

### P-Hacking
**Definition:** Believing in the significance of a statistical result without accounting for the number of comparisons or experiments run, only the significant ones being reported.

**Pattern:** `Many comparisons run; only the significant result reported; treated as if it were the only test performed.`

**Textbook example:** Testing 20 unrelated correlations and publishing only the one that was statistically significant by chance.

**Conversational example:** We tried a dozen subject lines and this one had the best open rate, so clearly this exact wording is what works.

### Garden of Forking Paths Fallacy
**Definition:** Incorrectly believing that a single experiment is immune to the multiple-comparisons problem because only one test was nominally run.

**Pattern:** `One experiment run with many possible analysis choices; result treated as if no multiple-comparisons risk existed.`

**Textbook example:** Analyzing one dataset multiple ways until a significant pattern emerges, then reporting it as a single planned test.

**Conversational example:** We only ran the analysis once — we just looked at it from a few different angles until something popped.

### Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy
**Definition:** Improperly asserting a cause to explain a cluster of data after the fact, ignoring that clusters can occur randomly.

**Pattern:** `Data shows a cluster C; a cause is retroactively proposed to explain C, without prior prediction.`

**Textbook example:** Noticing a cluster of cancer cases in one neighborhood and assuming a specific local cause, without statistical testing.

**Conversational example:** Three of our best hires all went to the same college, so that college must be where we should focus recruiting.

### Sunk Costs Fallacy
**Definition:** Continuing a course of action because of the time, money, or effort already invested, rather than its future value.

**Pattern:** `Invested resources R in X already; therefore continue with X (regardless of X's future prospects).`

**Textbook example:** Continuing to watch a bad movie because you already paid for the ticket.

**Conversational example:** We've already spent eight months on this feature, so we have to ship it even though no one wants it anymore.

## Informal: Argument from Ignorance / Silence

### Appeal to the Stone
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Lapidem*

**Definition:** Dismissing a claim as absurd without providing any proof or argument for why it is absurd.

**Pattern:** `X is absurd (asserted, not argued).`

**Textbook example:** Responding to a philosophical argument by simply kicking a stone and saying 'I refute it thus.'

**Conversational example:** That's ridiculous, I'm not even going to respond to that.

### Invincible Ignorance
*Also known as: Argument by Pigheadedness*

**Definition:** Refusing to accept an argument or evidence, no matter how strong, simply by continuing to disbelieve.

**Pattern:** `Present evidence E for X; responder rejects X regardless of E, repeatedly.`

**Textbook example:** No amount of fossil evidence will convince a strict young-earth advocate that the earth is billions of years old.

**Conversational example:** I don't care what the data says, I just don't believe it, and nothing will change that.

### Argument from Ignorance
*Also known as: Appeal to Ignorance, Argumentum ad Ignorantiam*

**Definition:** Assuming a claim is true because it hasn't been proven false, or false because it hasn't been proven true.

**Pattern:** `X has not been proven false; therefore X is true.`

**Textbook example:** No one has proven aliens don't exist, so they must exist.

**Conversational example:** You can't prove he didn't do it, so he probably did.

### Argument from Repetition
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Nauseam, Ad Infinitum*

**Definition:** Repeating an argument until objections cease, then treating the lack of continued objection as evidence of truth.

**Pattern:** `Restate X repeatedly; opposition eventually stops objecting; treat silence as agreement/proof.`

**Textbook example:** Restating a claim in every meeting until people stop pushing back, then citing that as consensus.

**Conversational example:** I've said this five times now and nobody's arguing anymore, so I guess we all agree.

### Argument from Silence
*Also known as: Argumentum ex Silentio*

**Definition:** Assuming a claim is true (or false) based on the absence of evidence or comment from an authoritative source.

**Pattern:** `Source S says nothing about X; therefore X is true/false.`

**Textbook example:** An ancient text doesn't mention a particular event, so historians conclude it never happened.

**Conversational example:** He never denied the rumor, so it's probably true.

## Informal: Relevance Fallacy

### Ignoratio Elenchi
*Also known as: Irrelevant Conclusion, Missing the Point*

**Definition:** Presenting an argument that may be internally valid but fails to address the actual question at issue.

**Pattern:** `Argument proves Y; the question at issue was X; Y is irrelevant to X.`

**Textbook example:** Arguing at length that crime is bad in response to a question about whether a specific policy reduces crime.

**Conversational example:** Asked if the budget overran, he gave a ten-minute answer about how hard the team worked.

### Red Herring
*Also known as: Chewbacca Defense, Dead Cat Strategy*

**Definition:** Introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention away from the original argument.

**Pattern:** `Discussing X; introduce unrelated Y to redirect the conversation away from X.`

**Textbook example:** If you want to complain about the dishes I left in the sink, what about the laundry you never do?

**Conversational example:** You're asking about the missed deadline, but let's talk about how the client changed requirements three times.

### Ad Hominem
**Definition:** Attacking the person making an argument rather than addressing the argument itself.

**Pattern:** `Person P makes argument A; attack P's character instead of A.`

**Textbook example:** You can't trust his economic plan, he's never even run a business.

**Conversational example:** Why would we listen to her about the budget, she's always been bad with money personally.

### Circumstantial Ad Hominem
**Definition:** Dismissing an argument because the arguer benefits from, or is biased toward, its conclusion.

**Pattern:** `P argues for X; P benefits from X; therefore X is false.`

**Textbook example:** Of course the tobacco company says cigarettes aren't that harmful — they profit from sales.

**Conversational example:** Of course he thinks remote work is fine, he wants to keep his home office setup.

### Poisoning the Well
**Definition:** Presenting adverse information about someone in advance, in order to discredit everything they will subsequently say.

**Pattern:** `Before P speaks, present negative info about P; audience discounts everything P says afterward.`

**Textbook example:** Before letting the witness testify, the lawyer mentions her past criminal record to bias the jury.

**Conversational example:** Before you hear his side, you should know he's lied to me before.

### Appeal to Motive
**Definition:** Dismissing an idea by questioning the motives of the person proposing it, rather than its substance.

**Pattern:** `P proposes X; P's motive for proposing X is questioned; therefore X is dismissed.`

**Textbook example:** She only supports the new policy because it benefits her department.

**Conversational example:** He's just saying that because he wants credit for the idea.

### Tone Policing
**Definition:** Focusing on the emotional tone of a message rather than its content, to discredit or dismiss it.

**Pattern:** `P makes claim C with emotional tone T; responder objects to T instead of addressing C.`

**Textbook example:** I might agree with you if you weren't so angry about it.

**Conversational example:** You'd have a better point if you didn't sound so frustrated right now.

### Traitorous Critic Fallacy
*Also known as: Ergo Decedo*

**Definition:** Portraying a critic's perceived affiliation as the real reason for their criticism, and demanding they recuse themselves entirely.

**Pattern:** `Critic C (affiliated with group G) criticizes X; response: C should have no say because of G.`

**Textbook example:** You used to work there, so your criticism of the company doesn't count.

**Conversational example:** You're not even on this team anymore, why are you weighing in on how we run sprints?

### Bulverism
*Also known as: Psychogenetic Fallacy*

**Definition:** Assuming an argument is wrong by speculating about the psychological reason someone holds it, without addressing the argument.

**Pattern:** `P argues X; explain why P psychologically believes X; conclude X is false.`

**Textbook example:** You only think that because you're insecure about your own choices.

**Conversational example:** You're just saying that because you're stressed about the deadline.

### Appeal to Authority
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Verecundiam*

**Definition:** Treating a claim as true solely because an authority figure asserts it, even outside their area of expertise or against the evidence.

**Pattern:** `Authority A says X; therefore X is true (regardless of evidence or relevance of A's expertise).`

**Textbook example:** A famous physicist says a particular diet is healthy, so it must be true.

**Conversational example:** Our CEO said this approach will work, and he's been right before, so it'll work.

### Appeal to Accomplishment
**Definition:** Judging a claim as true or false based on the accomplishments (or lack thereof) of the person making it.

**Pattern:** `P has accomplishment A; therefore P's claim X is true (regardless of evidence for X).`

**Textbook example:** She built a billion-dollar company, so her opinion on parenting must be correct.

**Conversational example:** He's published twenty papers, so obviously his take on this unrelated topic is right too.

### Courtier's Reply
**Definition:** Dismissing a critic by claiming they lack the credentials or deep knowledge required to comment, rather than engaging the criticism.

**Pattern:** `Critic C criticizes X; response: C hasn't studied X deeply enough to have an opinion.`

**Textbook example:** You haven't read all the theological texts, so your criticism of religion is invalid.

**Conversational example:** You haven't actually used the framework in production, so your critique of it doesn't count.

## Informal: Appeal to Emotion

### Appeal to Consequences
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Consequentiam*

**Definition:** Arguing that a claim must be true or false based on whether its consequences are desirable or undesirable.

**Pattern:** `If X is true, consequence C (bad) follows; therefore X is false.`

**Textbook example:** Free will can't be an illusion, because if it were, no one could be held morally responsible.

**Conversational example:** It can't be true that the project is behind schedule, because then we'd have to tell the client bad news.

### Appeal to Emotion
**Definition:** Manipulating an audience's emotions in place of presenting valid reasoning.

**Pattern:** `Evoke emotional response E; use E to obtain agreement with X (without evidence for X).`

**Textbook example:** Think of the children — how could you possibly oppose this law?

**Conversational example:** After everything this team has sacrificed, how could you even consider cutting the budget?

### Appeal to Fear
**Definition:** Generating fear, anxiety, or distress in the audience to obtain agreement, rather than presenting evidence.

**Pattern:** `If not X, then terrible outcome O; therefore X.`

**Textbook example:** If we don't pass this law, crime will spiral out of control.

**Conversational example:** If we don't approve this budget right now, the whole project could collapse.

### Appeal to Flattery
**Definition:** Using excessive or insincere praise to win agreement rather than relying on evidence.

**Pattern:** `Praise audience/listener; therefore listener should agree with speaker's claim.`

**Textbook example:** Someone as smart as you can clearly see why this plan is the right one.

**Conversational example:** You're way too sharp to fall for the other team's pitch — you'll see ours is the better choice.

### Appeal to Pity
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Misericordiam*

**Definition:** Generating sympathy or mercy in the listener as a substitute for actual evidence or justification.

**Pattern:** `P is in a sympathetic situation; therefore P's claim/request should be granted.`

**Textbook example:** I should pass this class — my whole semester has been so hard, with family issues and everything.

**Conversational example:** I know I missed the deadline, but it's been such a rough month, you have to understand.

### Appeal to Ridicule
**Definition:** Mocking or portraying an opponent's position as laughable rather than addressing its substance.

**Pattern:** `Position X is presented as absurd/ridiculous (mockingly), without addressing its merits.`

**Textbook example:** Oh sure, and next you'll tell me the moon is made of cheese.

**Conversational example:** Right, because that plan is just going to magically fix everything, sure.

### Appeal to Spite
**Definition:** Generating bitterness or hostility toward an opponent to win agreement, rather than presenting evidence.

**Pattern:** `Evoke resentment toward opponent O; use that resentment to support a conclusion against O's position.`

**Textbook example:** After everything they've done to us, how could you possibly side with them on this?

**Conversational example:** You really want to agree with the guy who threw you under the bus last quarter?

### Pooh-Pooh
*Also known as: Judgmental Language*

**Definition:** Dismissing an opponent's argument as unworthy of consideration, typically through derisive language, rather than refuting it.

**Pattern:** `Dismiss X as silly/unserious (without rebuttal).`

**Textbook example:** That theory isn't even worth discussing.

**Conversational example:** Yeah, that idea's not really worth our time.

### Style Over Substance
*Also known as: Rhyme-as-Reason Effect*

**Definition:** Judging an argument's validity by the eloquence or aesthetic appeal of its delivery rather than its content.

**Pattern:** `X is stated eloquently/memorably; therefore X is judged more true or persuasive.`

**Textbook example:** An ill caused is well excused (rhyming makes the claim feel more credible than it is).

**Conversational example:** He delivered that pitch so smoothly, it must be a solid plan.

### Wishful Thinking
**Definition:** Believing or arguing for something because it would be pleasant if true, rather than because of evidence.

**Pattern:** `X would be pleasant/desirable; therefore X is true.`

**Textbook example:** I just know everything will work out fine, so I'm not going to worry about the numbers.

**Conversational example:** I really feel like the client will love this without changes, so let's not bother testing it.

## Informal: Appeal to Tradition/Authority Bias

### Appeal to Nature
**Definition:** Judging something as good or bad solely based on whether it is 'natural' or 'unnatural.'

**Pattern:** `X is natural; therefore X is good (or X is unnatural; therefore X is bad).`

**Textbook example:** This supplement is all-natural, so it must be safe.

**Conversational example:** It's a totally organic process, so there's no way it could be harmful.

### Appeal to Novelty
*Also known as: Argumentum Novitatis*

**Definition:** Claiming a proposal is superior solely because it is new or modern.

**Pattern:** `X is new; therefore X is better than older alternatives.`

**Textbook example:** This is the latest management framework, so it must outperform the old one.

**Conversational example:** It's a brand-new tool, so it's bound to be better than what we're using now.

### Appeal to Poverty
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Lazarum*

**Definition:** Supporting a conclusion because the arguer is poor, or rejecting it because the arguer is wealthy.

**Pattern:** `P is poor; therefore P's claim X is true.`

**Textbook example:** He's struggled financially his whole life, so his investment advice must be trustworthy.

**Conversational example:** She grew up with nothing, so she clearly knows the right way to manage money.

### Appeal to Tradition
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Antiquitatem*

**Definition:** Supporting a conclusion solely because it has long been believed or practiced.

**Pattern:** `X has always been done/believed; therefore X is correct.`

**Textbook example:** We've always done performance reviews this way, so it must be the right approach.

**Conversational example:** This is how my parents raised me, so it's clearly the right way to raise kids.

### Appeal to Wealth
*Also known as: Argumentum ad Crumenam*

**Definition:** Supporting a conclusion because the arguer is wealthy, or rejecting it because the arguer is poor.

**Pattern:** `P is wealthy; therefore P's claim X is true.`

**Textbook example:** He's a billionaire, so his business advice must be correct.

**Conversational example:** She's clearly successful, so whatever strategy she's using must be the right one.

### Argumentum ad Baculum
*Also known as: Appeal to Force, Appeal to the Stick*

**Definition:** Supporting a position through threats or coercion rather than evidence.

**Pattern:** `Accept X, or face threat T; therefore accept X.`

**Textbook example:** Agree with the policy, or you'll be looking for a new job.

**Conversational example:** You'd better agree this was the right call, if you know what's good for your career here.

### Argumentum ad Populum
*Also known as: Bandwagon Argument, Appeal to the Majority*

**Definition:** Claiming a proposition is true or good solely because many or most people believe it.

**Pattern:** `Most people believe X; therefore X is true.`

**Textbook example:** Everyone is investing in this, so it must be a good investment.

**Conversational example:** Literally every other team has switched to this tool, so we should too.

### Association Fallacy
*Also known as: Guilt by Association, Honor by Association*

**Definition:** Arguing that because two things share, or are implied to share, a property, they must be the same or equally good/bad.

**Pattern:** `A is associated with B; B has property P; therefore A has property P.`

**Textbook example:** He supports the same policy as a disgraced politician, so his motives must be just as corrupt.

**Conversational example:** That vendor also works with a company we had problems with, so they're probably trouble too.

## Informal: Other Informal Fallacy

### Logic Chopping
*Also known as: Nit-Picking, Trivial Objections*

**Definition:** Focusing on minor, trivial details of an argument while ignoring its main point.

**Pattern:** `Argument A makes main point M with minor flaw F; response attacks only F, ignoring M.`

**Textbook example:** Dismissing an entire research paper's conclusion because of a single typo in a footnote.

**Conversational example:** You said 'about ten people' but it was actually eleven, so I don't think your whole point holds up.

### Ipse Dixit
*Also known as: Bare Assertion Fallacy*

**Definition:** Presenting a claim as self-evidently or dogmatically true without any supporting argument.

**Pattern:** `X is true (asserted, with no support given).`

**Textbook example:** That's just how it is.

**Conversational example:** It's obvious that this approach is correct, I don't need to explain why.

### Chronological Snobbery
**Definition:** Dismissing an idea as incorrect because it was popular at the same time as some other belief now known to be false.

**Pattern:** `Belief B was common alongside false belief F (in the same era); therefore B is also false.`

**Textbook example:** People in the past believed in spontaneous generation and also believed in classical logic, so classical logic must be outdated too.

**Conversational example:** That management style is from the same era as a lot of outdated office culture, so it must not work anymore.

### Fallacy of Relative Privation
*Also known as: Appeal to Worse Problems, Not as Bad as*

**Definition:** Dismissing a concern or complaint by pointing to a supposedly more important problem elsewhere.

**Pattern:** `X is a problem; but Y is a worse problem elsewhere; therefore X doesn't matter.`

**Textbook example:** Why worry about office snacks when there are people starving in the world?

**Conversational example:** You're upset about a delayed paycheck? People have real problems, you know.

### Genetic Fallacy
**Definition:** Judging a claim as true or false based solely on its origin, rather than its current merits.

**Pattern:** `X originated from source S; therefore X is true/false (based on S alone).`

**Textbook example:** That idea came from a rival company, so it must be a bad idea.

**Conversational example:** That suggestion came from the intern, so I doubt it's worth considering.

### I'm Entitled to My Opinion
**Definition:** Deflecting any challenge to a claim by asserting a right to hold the opinion, rather than defending its truth.

**Pattern:** `Challenge to claim X; response: I'm entitled to my opinion (avoiding the substance of the challenge).`

**Textbook example:** Well, that's just my opinion and I'm entitled to it.

**Conversational example:** I can think whatever I want about this, you don't have to agree.

### Moralistic Fallacy
**Definition:** Inferring factual claims about what is from premises about what ought to be, violating the fact-value distinction.

**Pattern:** `X ought to be true; therefore X is true.`

**Textbook example:** People shouldn't be naturally selfish, so human nature must not really be selfish.

**Conversational example:** Discrimination shouldn't affect outcomes, so it probably doesn't actually affect them much.

### Naturalistic Fallacy
*Also known as: Is-Ought Fallacy*

**Definition:** Inferring evaluative (ought) conclusions purely from factual (is) premises, violating the fact-value distinction.

**Pattern:** `X is the case; therefore X ought to be the case.`

**Textbook example:** Aggression occurs in nature, so aggression must be morally acceptable.

**Conversational example:** This is just how the industry has always operated, so that's how it should keep operating.

### Straw Man
**Definition:** Misrepresenting an opponent's argument in a weaker or distorted form, then refuting that distorted version instead of the original.

**Pattern:** `P argues X; misrepresent X as X' (weaker/distorted); refute X' as if it refuted X.`

**Textbook example:** Person A: We should regulate emissions. Person B: So you want to shut down all factories and destroy the economy?

**Conversational example:** I said we should review the budget more carefully. You're saying I want to cancel the whole project?

### Tu Quoque
*Also known as: Appeal to Hypocrisy, Whataboutism*

**Definition:** Dismissing a position by pointing out that its proponent fails to act consistently with it, rather than addressing the position itself.

**Pattern:** `P argues X is wrong; P has also done X; therefore X is not actually wrong / P's argument is invalid.`

**Textbook example:** You say smoking is bad, but you used to smoke yourself!

**Conversational example:** You're telling me to be on time, but you showed up late to the last three meetings.

### Two Wrongs Make a Right
**Definition:** Assuming that committing a second wrong act somehow cancels out or justifies the first.

**Pattern:** `Wrong act A was done to P; therefore P doing wrong act B in response is justified.`

**Textbook example:** They lied to us first, so it's fine that we lied to them.

**Conversational example:** They missed our deadline once, so it's only fair we miss theirs.

### Vacuous Truth
**Definition:** Making a claim that is technically true but meaningless because the relevant category it refers to is empty.

**Pattern:** `No A in B has property C, stated when there are no A in B at all.`

**Textbook example:** None of the unicorns in my garden are purple (true, because there are no unicorns at all).

**Conversational example:** None of our remote employees have complained about the office coffee (we have zero remote employees).

### Naturalistic Fallacy Fallacy
*Also known as: Anti-Naturalistic Fallacy*

**Definition:** Incorrectly inferring, from the general invalidity of the is-ought fallacy, that no factual premise can ever support any evaluative conclusion.

**Pattern:** `Is-ought inferences are generally invalid; therefore no is-statement can ever justify any ought-statement.`

**Textbook example:** Claiming that facts about suffering can never have any bearing on whether causing suffering is wrong.

**Conversational example:** You can't use any data about harm to argue this policy is wrong — that's just an is-ought fallacy, full stop.
